      title: "Research Program"
      text: |
        I am a life course sociologist who studies how social structures shape people's perceived possibilities, identities, decisions, and trajectories over time. I am especially interested in the transition to adulthood, when decisions about education, work, mobility, relationships, and family life can have lasting consequences for subsequent pathways.

        A central question connects my research: **How do people develop understandings of what is possible for their lives, and how are those possibilities shaped by the social contexts in which they are embedded?** I examine how institutions, families, social relationships, and unequal access to resources structure both the choices people encounter and how they interpret those choices. This perspective allows me to connect individual decision-making and identity development to broader social structures without treating either as independent of the other.

        I study these processes across several contexts, particularly education, migration, and family life. Although these settings differ, I see them as part of the same research program. My work on first-generation college students examines how institutional expectations, advising, peer relationships, and access to information shape students' experiences and educational trajectories. My research on migration and family life asks how resources, relationships, expectations, and changing social contexts influence young people's plans for education, work, migration, marriage, and family formation. My work on subjective adulthood similarly considers how people's understandings of themselves change alongside their expectations for adult roles and relationships.

        Across these studies, I am interested not simply in which outcomes people experience, but in the processes through which trajectories take shape. This requires attention to both social structure and change over time. I therefore use longitudinal and mixed-method approaches that allow me to examine how circumstances, relationships, identities, and priorities develop within individuals as well as how trajectories differ across social and institutional contexts.

      text: |
        My current research develops this broader agenda through several collaborative and independent projects.

        **First-Generation Student Study.** This mixed-method project examines how students navigate college over time. I focus particularly on how institutional expectations, advising structures, peer relationships, and access to information shape students' experiences and trajectories. Rather than locating differences in outcomes within students themselves, this work considers how colleges are organized around assumptions about resources, knowledge, and prior experience that are not equally distributed.

        **Family Migration and Early Life Outcomes (FAMELO).** This collaborative project uses longitudinal data from diverse social contexts to study young people's transitions toward adulthood. My work with these data examines migration aspirations, family migration, marriage preferences, family and non-family roles, and subjective adulthood. Across these topics, I ask how changing circumstances and relationships influence what young people anticipate for their futures and the decisions they make as they move toward adulthood.

        **Education policy and institutions.** I am extending my research on educational institutions into K–12 education through *From Policy to Practice: Administrator Perspectives on the LEARNS Act*. This project examines how Arkansas school administrators interpret policy change and translate it into organizational practice. It extends a recurring concern in my research by examining what happens between formal social structures and individual experiences—how institutional actors interpret broader conditions and, in doing so, help shape the environments that others encounter.

      title: "Longitudinal and Mixed Methods"
      text: |
        My methodological approach follows from my interest in how lives develop over time and within social contexts. I use longitudinal and mixed methods to connect population-level patterns with changes within individuals and with people's interpretations of their own experiences.

        My quantitative work includes longitudinal and panel-data analysis, regression-based approaches, multilevel modeling, latent-variable methods, and demographic analysis. My qualitative work uses in-depth interviews, coding and thematic analysis, and the integration of qualitative evidence with longitudinal survey data.

        I am especially interested in designs that allow me to study processes rather than outcomes alone. Longitudinal data make it possible to observe how circumstances, expectations, identities, and behaviors change together over time, while qualitative evidence helps explain how people understand those circumstances and make decisions within them. Combining these approaches allows me to connect lived experience with the larger social structures in which lives unfold.

      title: "Research Program"
      text: |
        I am a life course sociologist who studies how social structures shape young people's perceived possibilities, identities, decisions, and trajectories during the transition to adulthood.

        My work focuses especially on education, migration, and family life. I am interested in how institutions, social relationships, and broader social contexts shape the options people perceive as available to them and how those possibilities change over time. 

        Methodologically, I use longitudinal and mixed-method approaches. I am particularly interested in connecting within-person change and longer-term trajectories with the institutional and relational contexts in which they unfold.

        My current work is organized around two broad areas: **education, institutions, and inequality** and **migration, family, and the transition to adulthood**. These areas draw on several collaborative projects, including the **First-Generation Student Study** and **Family Migration and Early Life Outcomes (FAMELO)**, as well as newer research on education policy and subjective adulthood.

        For a full publication list and citation metrics, see [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=oBPE4ZgAAAAJ&hl=en).

      title: "Education, Institutions, and Inequality"
      text: |
        My research on education examines how institutional structures and everyday organizational practices shape students' experiences, opportunities, and longer-term pathways.

        A major strand of this work focuses on first-generation college students. I examine how advising structures, peer networks, academic expectations, and institutional assumptions shape students' experiences of college and their ability to navigate educational opportunities. Rather than treating differences in student outcomes as individual shortcomings, I focus on how institutions are organized around assumptions about resources, information, and prior experience that are not equally distributed.

        Much of this research draws on the **First-Generation Student Study**, a mixed-method project that combines longitudinal survey data with qualitative interviews to examine how students' experiences and trajectories develop over time.

        I am also extending this work beyond higher education. My current project, **From Policy to Practice: Administrator Perspectives on the LEARNS Act**, examines how school administrators interpret and respond to changes in Arkansas education policy. This project allows me to study how policy is mediated through local institutions and how organizational actors translate formal reforms into everyday practice.

      title: "Migration, Family, and the Transition to Adulthood"
      text: |
        My research on migration and family life examines how young people develop aspirations, identities, and plans for adulthood within changing social and family contexts.

        I am especially interested in how migration reshapes the resources, relationships, and perceived possibilities that young people draw on as they make decisions about education, work, mobility, marriage, and family life. Much of this work uses comparative longitudinal data to examine how life-course transitions unfold differently across institutional and cultural settings.

        A central source for this research is **Family Migration and Early Life Outcomes (FAMELO)**, a collaborative project examining how family migration shapes children's and adolescents' educational trajectories, wellbeing, aspirations, and family formation across distinct migration contexts.

      title: "Longitudinal and Mixed Methods"
      text: |
        My methodological approach reflects my substantive interest in how lives unfold over time. I use longitudinal and mixed methods to study both durable patterns across populations and changes within individuals.

        My quantitative work includes longitudinal and panel-data analysis, regression-based approaches, multilevel modeling, latent-variable methods, and demographic analysis. My qualitative work includes in-depth interviewing, coding and thematic analysis, and the integration of qualitative evidence with longitudinal survey data.

        I am particularly interested in research designs that connect lived experience with broader social patterns. Mixed-method and longitudinal approaches allow me to examine not only whether trajectories differ, but also how people interpret their circumstances, how their priorities change, and how institutions and relationships shape those changes.

        This methodological orientation also informs my teaching and undergraduate research mentoring, particularly in Social Statistics, Sociological Research Methods, and independent student research.
